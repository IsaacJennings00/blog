---
title: "Is Autocracy a Byproduct of Complexity?"
date: 2026-11-25
---

The question is more elaborately, do societies that grow more complex tend to develop stronger mechanisms of centralised control? High rates of technological innovation and diffusion, economic specialisation, and cultural interdependence raise the number of possible states a society can enter and the number of disturbances that can disrupt it.

[W. Ross Ashby’s Law of Requisite Variety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variety_(cybernetics)) holds that a regulator can only reduce the variety of outcomes it cares about if its own repertoire of responses is at least as large as the variety of disturbances it faces. Plainly, only variety absorbs variety. A complex society generates high variety through specialised occupations, long supply chains, rapid information flows, and dense interactions among strangers. A central authority that wants stable outcomes can either increase its own variety: through more personnel, more data, more specialised rules, more sensors. Alternatively, it can reduce the variety that reaches it by imposing standards, limiting options, and simplifying permitted behaviour through regulation. Interestingly, both responses enlarge the apparatus of control. An expansive state of *participation* in the former, or an expansive state of *regulation* in the latter.

Historical evidence demonstrates this pattern at various scales. Cross cultural and archaeological records indicate that increases in population size and hierarchical levels raise the upper bound on how concentrated power can become. Larger polities can sustain more administrative tiers, specialised officials, and information processing systems. Analysing premodern societies scale alone does not force autocracy, instead being dependent on the fiscal structure of that society. When a state extracts resources from many small, mobile producers, it must bargain and restrain itself. When it can monopolise external or easily controlled revenues such as income taxation, mines, globalised or even continental trade or coerced labor, autocratic arrangements emerge. Complexity supplies both the problems that invite central solutions and the administrative tools that make those solutions feasible.

[Joseph Tainter’s work](https://risk.princeton.edu/img/Historical_Collapse_Resources/Tainter_The_Collapse_of_Complex_Societies_ch_1_2_5_6.pdf_) on the collapse of complex societies supplies the complementary observation. Societies solve problems by adding layers of organisation, specialists, and control. Those additions raise costs while marginal returns decline. Collapse, in Tainter’s account, is the sudden loss of an established level of complexity: fewer hierarchical levels, less specialisation, reduced central coordination. That is, the point at which the energy and information required to maintain the control apparatus exceed what the system can reliably supply.

[Friedrich Hayek identified a related limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Use_of_Knowledge_in_Society) that appears once economies become highly specialised. The knowledge required to coordinate production is dispersed among millions of people and is often tacit knowledge of particular circumstances of time and place that cannot be fully articulated or transmitted in time for a central plan. Markets and local autonomy solve the coordination problem by allowing people to act on knowledge that no single mind possesses. Pure central direction faces an information bottleneck. Contemporary technology narrows that bottleneck. Large scale data collection, networked sensors, and machine learning make more previously local information legible and usable at a distance. The knowledge problem is not eliminated, but its practical severity is reduced for those who control the data infrastructure.

Modern digital systems illustrate the mechanism in real time. Surveillance technologies lower the cost of monitoring large populations. Facial recognition, location tracking, social-media analysis, and predictive tools allow authorities to detect patterns and identify individuals with far less human labor than earlier methods required. In China, procurement of AI facial-recognition systems has risen in localities with higher levels of unrest; subsequent reductions in visible protest have coincided with further investment in the same technologies. The loop is reinforcing: unrest prompts deployment, deployment improves capability and reduces open dissent, improved capability justifies continued or expanded use. Similar tools appear in democratic settings for narrower purposes—crime prevention, border control, public-health compliance—yet the underlying capacity is the same. Once the sensors and the analytical systems exist, the marginal cost of applying them to additional domains falls.

Regulatory expansion follows a parallel logic even without explicit political intent. Complex technologies create risks that are hard to contain at the local level: financial contagion, environmental externalities, supply-chain failures, data breaches. Agencies respond by writing rules that standardize practices, require reporting, and impose compliance obligations. Each rule reduces the variety of permitted behavior and increases the information that must flow to the center. Over time the body of rules grows because new problems continue to appear and because the existing apparatus is already positioned to absorb them. The process does not require a plan for total control; it requires only repeated decisions that the residual risk is unacceptable and that further standardization is the available remedy.

None of this makes authoritarian outcomes inevitable. [Polycentric arrangements](https://mcginnis.pages.iu.edu/Stephan%20Marshall%20McGinnis%20Intro%20to%20Polyc%20Gov.pdf), federated jurisdictions, and market processes can absorb substantial variety without concentrating decision rights in a single hierarchy. Exit options—geographic mobility, alternative providers, parallel institutions—raise the cost of over-centralisation. Institutional constraints that force rulers to negotiate with those who supply resources have historically limited the degree of autocracy even in large societies. The pressure remains, however. Each increment of technological and organisational complexity widens the set of disturbances that a society can generate and simultaneously improves the technical means available to a central controller. The combination makes expanded monitoring, standardisation, and hierarchical coordination the path of least resistance unless deliberate countermeasures are maintained.

The observable result is a gradual thickening of control capacity. Bureaucracies grow in size and specialisation. Data systems integrate previously separate streams of information. Rules multiply (or worse, exponentiate!). Rising complexity increases the demand for, and the technical feasibility of, more centralised mechanisms of coordination and restraint. Whether or not autocracy is realised, depends on many institutional checks and balance, but these too, can be eroded...
